# The Virginia Road Guide
## For Drivers 16 to 21, and the Parents Who Are Still Responsible for Them

*A Virginia-specific guide for families | August 2026*

---

## Why this guide exists

Virginia publishes excellent material for learner's permit holders and provisional drivers. The Department of Education's 45-Hour Parent/Teen Driving Guide is thorough and free, and every parent of a permit holder should download it.

But every official Virginia resource stops at the 18th birthday, because that is where the law stops. On that day, the passenger limit disappears, the midnight curfew disappears, and the hands-free phone ban relaxes to the adult standard.

Nothing about the driver changes on that day. The crash risk for 18 to 20 year olds remains among the highest of any age group. Your child moves from the most regulated period of their driving life to an unregulated one, usually at the same time they move away for college, start driving longer routes, and start driving late at night.

This guide covers the whole span, 16 to 21, and it is written for parents who are still the only meaningful safety system in place.

**Where to get the official materials:**

- 45-Hour Parent/Teen Driving Guide: doe.virginia.gov, Driver Education, Parent Resources
- Virginia Driver's Manual (English, Spanish, audio): dmv.virginia.gov
- Additional language translations, unofficial: reestablishrichmond.org/virginia-driving

# PART ONE - THE FOUR STAGES

## Stage 1: Learner's Permit (15 years 6 months and up)

**What the law requires**

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| Minimum age | 15 years, 6 months |
| Hold period | 9 months minimum before licensing |
| Supervised practice | 45 hours, of which 15 must be after sunset |
| Supervising driver | Licensed adult 21 or older, in the front passenger seat, at all times. A parent or guardian who is 18 or older also qualifies |
| Driver education | Required for anyone under 19 |
| Passengers | Only the supervising adult, plus family members |
| Phone | Prohibited entirely |
| Minimum licensing age | 16 years, 3 months |

**What matters more than the law**

The 45 hours are a floor, and treating them as a target is the single most common mistake parents make. Research consistently associates more supervised practice, in more varied conditions, with lower crash rates after licensing. The number that matters is not 45. It is closer to 100.

More important than the total is the *distribution*. Most families log 45 comfortable hours in daylight, in familiar neighborhoods, in good weather. That produces a teenager who is confident on the roads near home and helpless everywhere else.

**Deliberately log practice in each of these conditions before licensing:**

- [ ] Heavy rain, wipers on high
- [ ] Night, on an unlit road with no streetlights
- [ ] Highway merging in real traffic, not an empty ramp
- [ ] I-64, I-95, and I-295 interchanges, which are genuinely difficult
- [ ] A rural two-lane road at night, the highest-fatality road type in Virginia
- [ ] Parking garage, tight turns and steep ramps
- [ ] A route the teen navigates themselves while you say nothing
- [ ] Heavy traffic, Short Pump or Broad Street at 5:30 p.m.
- [ ] Fog
- [ ] Long drive of an hour or more, to build endurance
- [ ] Driving with a normal amount of background noise, so silence is not a crutch

**Coaching that works**

Follow the VDOE guide's sequence: parking lot, neighborhood, light traffic, rural highway, expressway, city. Do not skip forward because your child seems ready. Confidence arrives well before competence, and the gap between them is where crashes live.

Give directions early and calmly. "In about half a mile, we will turn right at the light" gives them time to plan. "Turn here!" teaches panic.

Do not narrate every mistake in the moment. Note it, stay quiet, and debrief in the driveway after the engine is off. Corrections shouted mid-maneuver create drivers who freeze.

End every session before either of you is frustrated. Twenty good minutes beats an hour that ends in an argument.

## Stage 2: Provisional License (16 years 3 months to 18)

Your child now drives alone. This is legal and expected. The restrictions govern who rides along and when, not whether an adult is present.

**What the law requires** (Code of Virginia § 46.2-334.01)

**Passengers.** No more than one passenger under age 21. After the license has been held one year, up to three passengers under 21, but only when driving to or from a school-sponsored activity, or with a licensed driver 21 or older in the front passenger seat, or in an emergency.

The family exemption covers spouse, children, stepchildren, siblings, half-siblings, **first cousins**, in-laws living in the household, and anyone cohabiting with the driver. It does **not** cover the children of family friends, however close those families are.

**Night driving.** No driving from midnight to 4:00 a.m., except to or from work, to or from an activity sponsored by a school or by a civic, religious or public organization and supervised by an adult, when accompanied by a parent in the front passenger seat, or in an emergency.

**Phone.** No handheld and no hands-free device use while driving, for any driver under 18. Stricter than the adult rule.

**Seat belts.** All occupants.

**The enforcement problem every parent needs to understand**

Virginia law states that police may not stop a vehicle solely because of a passenger or curfew violation. These are secondary offenses. An officer needs an independent reason to stop the car first.

In practice: if your child violates these rules, they will almost certainly not be caught. The state wrote the rules and then handed you the entire burden of enforcing them. If you do not enforce them, nobody does.

**The passenger rule is the one that saves lives**

Crash risk for a teen driver climbs sharply with each additional teen passenger, and the effect is well documented. It is not about the driver showing off, though that happens. It is that conversation, movement, and social pressure consume attention a new driver does not have to spare.

Your household rule should be stricter than the law. Zero non-family passengers for the first six to twelve months is a defensible and common family standard. Cousins are legally exempt but not safety-exempt. Treat them as passengers.

**The court licensing ceremony**

If your child is licensed before 18, the Juvenile and Domestic Relations District Court in your jurisdiction may require you both to attend a licensing ceremony to receive the license. You will get a notice by mail if so. Attend it seriously. A judge telling your child what a fatal crash file looks like carries weight that a parent's warning does not.

## Stage 3: The Eighteenth Birthday (the invisible cliff)

On this day, every restriction above disappears. Passenger limit, gone. Curfew, gone. Hands-free ban, relaxed to the adult standard.

Nothing about your child's driving ability changed overnight.

**What is actually true at 18 to 20:**

- They can legally drive five friends at 2 a.m.
- They typically leave for college, encountering entirely new roads and long unfamiliar drives home
- They start driving tired, after late study nights and shift work
- They encounter alcohol in social settings for the first time in a serious way, whether or not they drink
- Their crash risk stays elevated well past 20

**Zero tolerance still applies until 21.** Any measurable blood alcohol content for a driver under 21 is a criminal offense in Virginia, not a lesser infraction. Not "over the limit." Any amount.

**What replaces the law**

Nothing, unless you build it. Suggested family rules for 18 to 21, negotiated rather than imposed, because they are legal adults now:

- Passenger limit stays in place voluntarily during the first year of college
- Text or call on arrival for any drive over an hour
- No driving after fewer than six hours of sleep, non-negotiable
- Late-night drives home from campus happen the next morning instead
- Telematics or a driver-monitoring app stays on, and you review it together without turning it into an interrogation
- The safe-ride clause below never expires

**The conversation to have, once, out loud**

Your child will be in a car at some point with a driver who has been drinking. Statistically, this is close to certain. Decide together, now, exactly what they do in that moment. If the plan is "call us, any hour, no consequences," say it explicitly and then honor it the first time it is used. A single angry response the first time destroys the clause permanently.

## Stage 4: Driving Home for the Holidays (the specific danger)

The most dangerous drives your child will make between 18 and 21 are usually:

- The drive back to Richmond after finals, exhausted, at night
- The drive back to campus on a Sunday evening
- Any long unfamiliar route driven alone for the first time
- Late-night drives after social events

**Family protocol worth adopting:**

- Long drives happen in daylight, planned in advance, not decided at 9 p.m.
- The route is driven with a parent once before it is driven alone
- Departure and arrival texts, every time
- A stop every two hours on drives over three hours
- If they are too tired to drive, they stay the night and drive in the morning. Missing a class or a family event is not a reason to drive tired. Say this in advance so they do not have to ask.

# PART TWO - WHAT ACTUALLY CAUSES TEEN CRASHES

Understanding the mechanism matters more than a list of warnings.

**Inexperience, not recklessness.** The largest single factor is simply that the driver has not yet encountered enough situations to recognize them early. An experienced driver reads a road and predicts what will happen. A new driver reacts after it has already happened. This is why practice volume and variety beat lectures.

**Speed.** Not just illegal speed. Legal speed that is wrong for the conditions - a posted 55 in heavy rain, a familiar curve taken at the same speed as always but at night. Speed compresses the time available to recognize and react, which is exactly the resource a new driver is shortest on.

**Unfamiliar roads.** A driver who is navigating is not fully driving. Unfamiliar roads produce late braking, missed signs, sudden lane changes, and last-second turns. This is why the guide above insists on driving new routes with a parent first.

**Passengers.** Covered above. The strongest single modifiable factor available to you.

**Night.** Reduced visibility combines with fatigue and with the social contexts that occur at night. Virginia's rural two-lane roads at night are the highest-risk environment in the state.

**Phones.** Reading one message at highway speed means traveling the length of a football field without looking at the road.

**Thrill seeking.** Real, but often over-emphasized relative to the four factors above. It is also the least responsive to lectures and the most responsive to structural limits: passenger rules, telematics, and knowing that a parent will actually check.

**Fatigue.** Consistently underestimated. Reaction impairment from serious sleep deprivation is comparable to alcohol impairment. High schoolers running on five hours of sleep during exam season are driving impaired, and nobody calls it that.

# PART THREE - WHAT PARENTS GET WRONG

## Virginia rules that adults routinely misremember

Your child will learn these correctly in driver education. Many parents never did, or learned them decades ago in another state, and then model the wrong version every day.

- **Four-way stops.** First to arrive goes first. Ties go to the driver on the right.
- **Right turn on red.** Legal after a complete stop, unless posted otherwise. A complete stop means the wheels stop moving.
- **School buses.** Stop when the bus has red lights flashing and the stop arm extended, in **both** directions, unless separated by a median or barrier. Penalties are severe.
- **Move-over law.** Change lanes away from a stopped emergency vehicle. If you cannot, slow substantially.
- **Roundabouts.** Increasingly common in Henrico and across Virginia. Yield to traffic already circulating.
- **Reckless driving.** In Virginia this is a criminal misdemeanor, not a ticket, at 20 mph over the limit or at any speed above 85 mph. A teenager doing 86 on I-64 has committed a crime with a permanent record. Very few families know this.

## The thing that matters more than anything else in this guide

Your child has been watching you drive since they were small. Whatever you actually do - roll through stops, check your phone at red lights, drive 15 over on I-295, tailgate when you are late - is what they learned, regardless of what you have told them.

Sixteen years of watching outweighs one conversation. If you want to change one thing to protect your child, change your own driving first. It is more effective than anything else here and it costs nothing.

## Give your child permission to refuse

Teenagers are frequently asked to drive when they should not: when they are exhausted, when the weather is bad, when they do not know the route, or when someone needs a ride and they are the only one available. Saying no to an adult is hard for most teenagers.

Tell your child directly, and where possible in front of other adults, that they may always refuse to drive. Then back them when they do.

The same applies to passengers. If your household limit is one passenger, your child needs to be able to say "that is my parents' rule" and have the conversation end there. If you undercut that rule once because it was inconvenient, it is gone.

# PART FOUR - THE PARENT TOOLKIT

**Before licensing**

- [ ] Download the VDOE 45-Hour Parent/Teen Driving Guide and actually use the lesson sequence
- [ ] Aim well past 45 hours; 70 to 100 is a better target
- [ ] Cover every condition on the checklist in Stage 1
- [ ] Notify your insurance agent when the 180-day temporary license is issued
- [ ] Sign a written parent-teen driving agreement before the first solo drive

**At licensing**

- [ ] Set family rules stricter than the law, in writing, with review dates
- [ ] Enable telematics through your insurer or the vehicle, and be transparent that you are doing it
- [ ] Agree the safe-ride clause out loud, and mean it
- [ ] Confirm the car is roadworthy - tires, brakes, wipers, lights - and teach your child to check it

**Ongoing**

- [ ] Review the driving agreement at 30 days, 90 days, 6 months, and 1 year
- [ ] Loosen restrictions as they are earned, so the agreement is a ladder and not a cage
- [ ] Keep driving with them occasionally, even after licensing, especially before any new route
- [ ] Respond to a ticket or a near miss with a conversation and a restriction, not rage. Rage produces a child who hides things

**Skills nobody teaches in Virginia driver education**

Panic braking, skid recovery, wheel drop-off recovery, and collision avoidance are not part of the standard curriculum. B.R.A.K.E.S. (putonthebrakes.org) runs free hands-on training for teens 15 to 19 with a permit or license, plus a parent, and holds events at Richmond Raceway. This is the highest-value single day available to a new driver in this region.
